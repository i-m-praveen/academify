Moving an Olympiad from paper to a computer-based test makes it easier to deliver, score, and report on. It also raises the question every organizer and participating school will ask: can the results be trusted?

## Before exam day

Most integrity problems are prevented long before candidates log in:

- Build the paper from a question bank organized by subject, topic, and difficulty, so it can be balanced and reviewed properly
- Split the paper into sections, and decide your marking scheme — including negative marking — up front
- Turn on question and option shuffling so neighbouring candidates don't see the same paper in the same order
- Publish the test only to the classes, sections, or candidates who are registered for it

## During the sitting

Remote proctoring works best as a set of layered checks rather than a single barrier:

- **Webcam snapshots** at regular intervals, with checks for no face or more than one face in frame
- **Fullscreen enforcement** and **tab-switch detection**, so leaving the exam window is recorded
- **Flags for dev tools, print-screen, page refresh, and network drops**, each logged against the candidate

A live monitoring view lets your team follow every candidate's progress and violations as they happen, instead of discovering problems after the sitting has closed.

## After the sitting

Review flagged candidates first, then finalize results. Finalizing locks in scores and computes ranks across the whole cohort, which is what participating schools will want to see: a ranking, a section-wise breakdown, and a report they can export to Excel for their own records.
